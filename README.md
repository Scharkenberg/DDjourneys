# DDjourneys

DDjourneys is a journey planner for buses, trams and trains in and around Dresden. You tell it where you are going and when, and it shows you how to get there, what is late, and where your vehicle is right now. It runs on Android phones and on Windows.

I wrote it because I wanted something like the old Android app Öffi, but made for the timetable data of the Verkehrsverbund Oberelbe (VVO) and in a shape that fits current phones and works on Windows too. It is not an official app of the VVO or the DVB, and it is not made or actively endorsed by either of them.

## Getting started

Open the app and you land on the planner. Tap the field for the start and type a few letters, for example "Hauptbahnhof". Pick the stop from the list. Do the same for the destination, say "Hellerau". Press the search button and you get a list of journeys.

On a phone the app has a tab bar at the bottom with four tabs: the planner, departures, the map, and a fourth tab with the settings and everything behind them. On Windows there are no tabs; there everything opens from the planner.

If you are standing at the start, tap the small location symbol next to the start field instead of typing. The app asks for permission to use your position and then fills in the stop nearest to you. If you would rather start from the exact address you are at, there is a switch for that under Settings, in the place search section.

Below the fields you can change the time. The row of buttons moves it back an hour, back a quarter of an hour, to now, forward a quarter of an hour or forward an hour. Next to the time you choose whether you want to leave at that time or arrive by it. If you have to be at work at 8:30, choose "Arrive by", set 8:30, and the app works backwards.

Stops, addresses and sights all work as start or destination. In the settings you can switch addresses and sights off if you only want stops.

## The list of journeys

Each journey in the list shows the times, the stops, and a row of small tags with the lines you ride. Swipe that row sideways if it does not fit. At the bottom of the list you can ask for earlier or later journeys. The bookmark symbol saves the route, so you find it again at the top of the planner under saved routes. Routes you searched for before are listed there too.

When your start is an address, a point of interest or your position, you can switch on "Bike to the stop" under route preferences. The app then looks for stops within a radius you choose, plans a real bike route to each of them, and prefers the one with the least uphill among the rides that take a comparable time. The bike ride becomes the first part of the journey, shown with its distance, its hills and its riding time, and the first vehicle leaves only once you have parked the bike. When no bike route can be planned, the journey starts as an ordinary one and the app says so; a start that is itself a stop gets no bike part, because you board where you are.

## One journey in detail

Tap a journey to see it as a timeline. Every ride has a line, a direction, the stops where you get on and off, the platform, and the delay if there is one. Dots next to a time show how full the vehicle is, when the provider knows. At a change the app tells you how long you wait, whether you have to walk to another stop, and when the connection is tight or already lost.

Below the line of each ride there are up to three buttons. "Earlier" and "Later" look for another vehicle for that ride alone and keep the rest of your journey as far as it still fits; where it no longer does, the rest is planned again. The button with the map pin opens a map with the vehicle of this ride on it. That works for lines with a plain number, such as tram 11 or bus 61, and only when somebody has reported the position of the vehicle, so a vehicle can be missing even though it is running.

The route at the top of the page stays in place while the rest scrolls. Its icons refresh the journey, share it as text or as a picture and, if you switched on the technical details under Developer options, open them. Pulling the page down refreshes it too: the app asks the provider again and shows the same connection with its current times, or tells you that the provider no longer offers it. The bell in the card below follows the journey, which is explained further down. The other symbols there open it as a PDF or hand it to another app. The map symbol shows the whole route on a map without following a vehicle. Tickets and prices are at the bottom; the app shows the single and day ticket the provider quotes for this journey, and nothing else, because the provider quotes nothing else. Where the transport authority names a sale page, the price row can open it, and for the VVO it opens the authority's page about its mobile and online tickets; the app itself sells nothing. The TRIAS interface quotes no prices, so the app looks the same connection up at the VVO interface and shows its tickets when it finds it.

Above the tickets sits the ticket advice, a card of what the quoted prices say: how many fare zones the journey crosses, from which ride a day ticket becomes cheaper than single tickets, and when a Deutschlandticket would have paid for itself on this route at its monthly price. Under route preferences, Tickets, you can say that you have a Deutschlandticket; what the provider then prices by its own fare zones is shown as already paid for - the fares struck through, without buy buttons - and a journey over a long-distance train gets the warning that the ticket does not cover it. The BahnCard 25 line among the tickets is the app's own estimate, labelled as such, because the provider quotes no conditional prices. When a journey uses an on-demand service (the alita lines), a booking card sits at that ride: its link opens the operator's own booking information when the timetable data carries one, otherwise [the public page of the transport authority about on-demand rides](https://www.dvb.de/de-de/fahrplan/nachtverkehr/anruflinientaxi). The app books nothing itself.

## Departures

Open departures from the planner or from its tab on a phone and choose a stop. You get the next departures with their delays. Switch to arrivals if you are waiting for someone. Tap a departure to see all stops of that vehicle's run. A row of chips above the list narrows it down to the lines you pick: tap a chip to switch a line on or off, hold it to see only that line, and the chip at the end shows the services that were cancelled, which are otherwise hidden. Under "Stops near you" every stop shows the line and the minutes of its next departure; tap that strip to see up to three. The section "Around this stop" opens a map of the surroundings, tells you how accessible the stop is according to the city, and lists the lines that serve it; the map button next to a line shows where that line actually goes, in both directions. On the page of one run, a second button beside the map shows the whole line instead of just that one run. A line number also works as a search: type "11" in the place search and the app offers that line, with its course on the map, once it has seen it at a stop. The app keeps the last boards of the stops you looked at on the phone, so when a board cannot be loaded you still see it, honestly stamped with the time it was fetched. The share symbol next to the stop's name opens a sheet with a QR code that puts the stop on any map, and a message text that carries two links: the map link for every phone and the board link that opens this stop's departures in DDjourneys. The same sheet comes from a tap on a stop on the map and on the fare zone plan.

## Disruptions

The disruptions page lists what the VVO reports: construction work, diversions, cancelled services. Tap an entry to read it. Entries that the journey planner takes into account are marked as such. Lines you ride often are worth watching: the star beside a line under "Around this stop" and the star in the header of a run page put the line on your list, and its changes then lead the disruptions page in their own section, so you do not have to look for them. On Android the app also checks in the background while it is closed and puts up a notification when the disruptions of one of your lines change; on Windows the check runs while the app is open. The planner also says when one of your lines is affected, and the row in the More tab shows the count; you can send the planner note away for this session, and it comes back when something new happens. The line-course map names the stops the current run does not serve and shows the notice of the line; the data carries no diversion path, so none is drawn.

## Following a journey

If you press the bell on a journey, the app watches it for you. Shortly before you leave it sends a notification, and it sends another when something changes, such as a delay that makes you miss a connection or a vehicle that is cancelled. While you are on the way, a notification shows the next stop and when to get off, and counts the stops down: three stops to go, exit in about four minutes. A get-off alarm can ring shortly before your stop; under Settings, Journey options you choose whether it rings one, two or three minutes before, or never, and whether it rings with a sound or gently. When your plan says to change at a stop but the vehicle you are on also reaches your destination later on its own course, the app says so: stay in the vehicle. When a change only works if you walk faster than the pace you chose in the route preferences, the journey calls that change tight at your pace. When a connection is missed, the notification can open a new search at once, from the stop where it broke to your destination. You can pause or stop following at any time. The page "Followed journeys" lists everything you follow, with the lines as the same coloured tags as in the list of journeys; the ride you are on has an outline in the accent colour, and its stops can be opened as a course. On the map of a followed run you can switch on your own position: while the vehicle reports nothing, your device position is snapped onto the stops of the run and shown as a modest dot that says you are probably here, an estimate and never more. The switch asks for nothing; it works only while the app already has permission for your location, and the dot gives way as soon as the vehicle reports again.

Following works with the VVO provider only. The app hands the journey to a service run by the DVB, which keeps watching it. That service gives the app an anonymous token instead of an account. There is no login, and I never see who you are.

## Map

Under Settings, Map you choose which engine draws the map. CARTO is the sharp vector map in the colours of the app. It needs a key from CARTO, the company that makes the map background, and a phone whose web view can run WebGL 2. The app does not come with a key of its own. You get one on the CARTO website and paste it under Settings, Map. Leaflet is the simple map. It shows ready-made picture tiles from OpenStreetMap, needs no key and runs on old phones too, but only light and dark follow the theme of the app. Both engines show the same journeys, stops and vehicles, and on the map that the map symbol in the header of the planner opens (also under Settings, Map), the layers button at the top right can show the tariff zones of the VVO, the park and ride sites with how many spaces are free, the shared bikes and e-scooters and the vehicles moving around right now, and opens two plans: the fare zone plan and the line network map. Under Settings, Offline you can download the picture tiles of the simple map for Dresden and its close outskirts once; when the network then does not deliver a tile, the map answers from the device and says so with a small honest note. The vector map keeps needing its own connection, and tiles deeper than street level keep coming from the network whenever there is one.

The line network map is the DVB's plan of Dresden, drawn from the PDF the DVB publishes. It is drawn piece by piece, as sharp as the zoom asks for, so even the smallest print stays clear. It is downloaded once a week and then also works offline. The row of chips at the top switches between four DVB plans: the standard network plan, the night plan of the GuteNachtLinien, the network plan for passengers with reduced mobility (a PDF only, without a picture as its fallback), and the MOBI city map for them. Each is downloaded and kept like the standard one, and the document button opens the chosen plan's PDF in another app.

The fare zone plan is the interactive plan of the VVO website in the app: the fare zones of the whole VVO area with every line on them, and a second plan of Dresden, switched at the top. Zoom in and every stop can be tapped, for its departures or a journey to or from it; a tap while zoomed out zooms in towards it. The search button finds a stop on the plan by name, the location button marks the stop nearest to you, and on a computer the name of a stop shows when the pointer rests on it. The plan is kept on the device once you have seen it, and on Wi-Fi the rest of it is fetched in the background, so it works offline too. In the details of a journey, below the tickets, "Show on the fare zone plan" opens it with the start and the end of the journey marked, so you can see the zones the ticket counts.

If the phone cannot draw the CARTO map, the app says so instead of showing an empty square, and offers to use Leaflet instead or to try anyway. Trying anyway can end the app. If that happens the app remembers it and keeps the CARTO map off until you try again or save the key again. Everything else in the app works without a map.

When the map shows the surroundings, stops appear once you zoom in. Tap a stop to see its departures or to plan a journey to or from it.

A park and ride site shows how many of its spaces are free right now, green while there is room, amber when it is filling up and red when it is full. A site that does not count its cars shows only how many spaces it has, in grey.

The bikes layer shows the MOBIbike stations of Nextbike in two shapes. A square is a MOBIpunkt, a real station with docks. A dashed circle is a virtual station, an area where you leave the bike without a dock. The number on a station is how many bikes are there right now, and tapping it tells you how many docks are free and how many cargo bikes there are. Zoom in a little further and the bikes standing free appear as small discs: Lime's e-bikes in green and MOBIbikes left in the street in blue. Tap a station or a bike to plan a journey to it, or to rent it: on Android the app opens Nextbike or Lime right at that station or bike, or their page in the Play Store if the app is not installed yet. Voi also rents e-bikes and e-scooters in Dresden, but publishes no open data for it, so the map cannot show them.

## Android widgets, quick actions and Windows widgets

On Android you can put widgets on your home screen: a route you travel often, the departures or arrivals of one stop, the stops around you with their distance, or the next departures from the stops around you. Each widget has its own settings, such as its provider, its title and how many rows it shows. A tap on a widget refreshes it. Android allows widgets to update every thirty minutes at most, and widgets in the background use the last position the phone knows unless you allow location access all the time.

Press and hold the app icon to find two quick actions. "Take me home" plans a journey from the stop nearest to you to your home stop. "Departures from here" shows the departures at the stop nearest to you. You set your home in the planner: tap the home button above the list of recent places, or the pencil next to it once a home is set, and search for a stop, an address or a place the way you do for a start. On Windows the same actions are in the jump list of the app.

Windows has its own widgets board. There the app offers two kinds of widget: the departures of a stop and the next journeys of a route. You pin them from the board itself, and their settings are made in the app; the widget can also send you straight to the departures of its stop or the plan of its route. While the app runs, the widgets are refreshed every thirty minutes, and when the board asks for them it is served the last picture it was given. When the app is fully closed there is no background work: the board shows the last picture and says so, and which widgets are offered at all varies by the Windows region and settings.

## Settings

Under Appearance you choose light, dark, the system setting, or the times of sunrise and sunset as the switch between light and dark (computed for the provider's city; no location permission), plus a colour set, a font, and how tightly the screens are packed. On Windows you can also choose a window material: Mica is the calm one and stays close to the colours of the app, Mica Alt is the more pronounced one and lets more of the wallpaper and the accent colour through. Language is German, English or Czech. Under Appearance you also choose the size of the text (normal, large, or largest - the rows grow with it, so nothing gets clipped) and the colours of the transport modes: the standard ones, or one of three sets that stay tell-apart for a kind of colour vision (deuteranopia, protanopia, tritanopia), so no line ever hides behind another. The switch "Large & simple" is a starting point for big reading: it sets the largest text, the roomy packing and journey cards reduced to the time and only the next step, in huge type; every one of those settings stays adjustable on its own. Route preferences cover the kinds of vehicles, how many changes you accept, how fast you walk, what you need for accessibility, and how long the journey should stay at a stop over; from three minutes on, journeys that only pass through the stop over drop out. Options that the selected provider cannot use do not appear. The get-off alarm of a followed journey is set under Journey options too: one, two or three minutes before your stop, or off, and with or without a sound. Under Tickets you can say that you have a Deutschlandticket, which turns the ticket advice of a journey on. The provider page lets you switch between the VVO and the TRIAS interface of the VVO. The second one is marked as experimental because it does less and I have tested it less. Under Offline the city map download waits for Wi-Fi by default and shows an estimate before it starts; the same section tells you that the departure boards of your stops are kept on the phone.

Under Start you can make the app open with the start already filled in and the destination search ready, so that a trip home takes two taps. On a phone you can also choose which screen the app opens on, the planner or the map.

## Using it from other apps

Other apps can open DDjourneys with a link, for example `ddjourneys://go?to=Hellerau` plans a journey to Hellerau starting now. Any app that sends a map location, a `geo:` link, can hand the place to DDjourneys on Android. The details for developers are in `docs/EXTERNAL_CONTRACT.md`.

## Where the data comes from

Journeys, departures and disruptions come from the interfaces of the VVO. Following a journey uses the DVB service mentioned above. Live positions come from the TLMS community network, which collects radio telegrams and GPS positions, so it is incomplete by nature. The live vehicles on the plain map come from the same network. Accessibility of stops and service points come from the open data of the city of Dresden. The park and ride sites with their free spaces come from the open data of the VVO. The shared bikes come from the open data of Nextbike (MOBIbike) and Lime. The bike routes of the feeder come from the public BRouter service. The map background comes from CARTO and OpenStreetMap, drawn by the open source libraries MapLibre and Leaflet. The network plans of the line network map page (standard, night, barrier-free) are downloaded from the DVB website, the fare zone plans from the VVO website; all rights to them stay with the DVB and the VVO. The timetable data belongs to the VVO and the DVB. The one-time city map download under Settings, Offline fetches its picture tiles from tile.openstreetmap.org and, when that server does not answer, from the raster tiles of CARTO at basemaps.cartocdn.com — the same public tile servers the map page loads from whenever it shows a map.

## Privacy

The app has no account, no advertising and no analytics. It keeps your settings, places and routes on your phone. Your position is used only when you ask for it, when a widget or a quick action needs it, and is sent to the VVO as a coordinate to find the nearest stop. The dot on the map of a followed run stays on your phone; it is not sent anywhere. Searches go to the VVO as you type. Nothing is sent to me. Downloading the offline city map calls the public tile servers of OpenStreetMap and CARTO from your phone, the same ones the map itself loads from; nothing else leaves the phone for it. When the bike feeder is switched on, the points of its route request, your start and the stop you board at, go to the BRouter service at brouter.de, which plans the bike track.

## When something does not work

Under Settings you find Developer options. If you switch them on you can record a log file that lists every request the app makes and what came back. Delete it when you are done. The log starts with the version of the app and the version of every interface it talks to, which makes it much easier to find the cause. If you report a problem on the project page on GitHub, github.com/Scharkenberg/DDjourneys, attach the log and say what you did. (The app does not log actions or user data otherwise.)
